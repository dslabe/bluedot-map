Layer 1
Corporate & Voluntary Commitments
Most active, least enforceable
▼
12 companies have published frontier safety policies (per METR). All 12 include deployment mitigations, accountability mechanisms, and update commitments. But these are voluntary. No external body verifies compliance, and competitive pressure incentivizes corner-cutting.
Frontier AI Safety Policies
Self-governance mechanism
Published by Anthropic, OpenAI, Google DeepMind, Meta, xAI, Microsoft, Amazon, and others. Define capability thresholds, deployment mitigations, and halt conditions. Vary widely in specificity and ambition.
No external verificationVoluntary compliance
Internal Safety Teams
Corporate function
Red teams, preparedness teams, alignment researchers. Do real technical work but are subordinate to leadership decisions. At OpenAI, SAG advises but CEO makes final go/no-go calls.
Depends on: CEO authorityCan be overridden
Seoul Safety Commitments
Voluntary international pledge
20 companies committed to publish safety policies, conduct pre-deployment testing, and share risk information. Prompted the wave of published frameworks. No enforcement mechanism exists.
Depends on: reputational pressureNo penalties for noncompliance
Open-Weight Model Releases
Distribution mechanism
DeepSeek, Llama, Qwen. Safeguards easily removed post-download. Once released, weights can't be recalled. Gap to closed models now under 1 year. Great for research access, hard to govern.
Irreversible once releasedSafeguards strippable
Layer 2
National Regulation & State Technical Capacity
Growing but fragmented
▼
A few jurisdictions are beginning to formalize requirements. But per Brundage, existing laws have fatal flaws, and there's no jurisdiction where a company must prove its safety policy actually works.
EU AI Act + GPAI Code
Regulation — EU
Most comprehensive binding framework. GPAI Code of Practice covers safety, security, systemic risk tiers, deployment conditions. But enforcement is still ramping up and vulnerable to political pressure.
Depends on: enforcement capacityDepends on: geopolitical stability
US: SB 53 + RAISE Act
Legislation — US
SB 53 requires publishing a safety framework, but it can be "garbage" (Brundage). RAISE Act adds "detailed" requirement starting 2027. No external auditing mandate. Federal preemption EO contested in courts.
No audit requirementWatered down by lobbyingWindow: post-midterms 2027
China: Framework 2.0 + TC260
Standards body — China
Non-binding guidance, but China translates frameworks into enforceable standards fast. Covers CBRN, open-source risks, loss of control. Risk categorization standard already in drafting. Content control remains the primary binding driver.
Depends on: CAC enforcementFeeds into: binding tech standards
UK AISI
Government institute — UK
World-leading technical capacity for model testing and safety research. Trusted voluntary partnerships with companies. But no legislation to back it up. UK frontier AI bill has stalled twice under two governments.
Depends on: voluntary company accessNo legislative mandateFeeds into: Intl AISI Network
US NIST / CAISI
Government institute — US
AI Risk Management Framework (non-binding). CAISI partnership with UK AISI. Technical capacity exists but politically constrained under current administration's pro-acceleration stance.
Depends on: administration prioritiesFeeds into: Intl AISI Network
Layer 3
International Coordination & Multilateral Efforts
High convening, low teeth
▼
International mechanisms generate shared knowledge and political pressure but lack enforcement power. The "AI race" narrative actively undermines cooperation by framing safety as a competitive disadvantage.
Intl AI Safety Report
Scientific assessment
Chaired by Bengio, 100+ experts, 30+ countries. Provides shared evidence base. Explicitly does not recommend policies. Second edition (Feb 2026) significantly expanded risk coverage.
Feeds into: national policymakingInformational only
Intl AISI Network
Coordination network
UK as Network Coordinator. Facilitates info-sharing between national AI safety institutes. Recently renamed. No binding authority over member states.
Depends on: member state capacityDepends on: US-UK relationship
AI Safety Summits
Diplomatic forum
Bletchley (2023), Seoul (2024), Paris (2025). Got US and China in the same room. Produced declarations and voluntary commitments. Impact depends entirely on follow-through.
Declarations ≠ actionSparked: Seoul commitments
OECD AI Observatory
Monitoring & data
Runs the AI Incidents Monitor (10,000+ incidents tracked). Provides data infrastructure that other actors depend on. No regulatory power but shapes the evidence base used globally.
Feeds into: Intl Safety ReportFeeds into: national policy
Layer 4
Civil Society, Research & Auditing
Growing fast, still undersized
▼
Third-party evaluators and researchers are critical but face two bottlenecks: companies often won't share non-public information, and there aren't enough qualified auditors to cover even the top dozen companies on a narrow set of risks (Brundage).
METR
Evaluation org
Model Evaluation & Threat Research. Conducts dangerous capability evaluations. Tracks and compares frontier safety policies. Key source of technical evidence for policymakers.
Depends on: company cooperationFeeds into: safety policy design
AVERI
Auditing advocacy
Brundage's org. Pushing for mandatory third-party frontier AI auditing. Building the intellectual infrastructure for what auditing should look like. Needs a regulatory demand signal to scale.
Depends on: regulatory mandatesEcosystem undersized
AI Super PACs
Political spending — US
Both pro-acceleration ("Leading the Future") and pro-regulation PACs. Reshaping which candidates support or oppose AI legislation. Significant and growing spending heading into 2026 midterms.
Shapes: US legislative landscapeDepends on: election cycles
Academic Safety Research
Research ecosystem
Alignment, interpretability, evaluation science. Still "mom and pop" scale relative to capabilities research. Stymied by limited model access and high cost of independent replication.
Underfunded vs. capabilitiesLimited model access
Policy Orgs (CLTR, Carnegie, etc.)
Policy research
Generate analysis, cross-border understanding, and concrete proposals. Reduce information asymmetries. Influence is indirect, through policymakers and public discourse.
Feeds into: government thinkingFeeds into: public debate

Critical Dependencies & Structural Gaps

→
The verification gap: No jurisdiction requires external auditing of whether companies follow their own safety policies. You must publish a policy (SB 53), but nobody checks if you follow it.
→
The evaluation gap: Pre-deployment tests don't reliably predict real-world performance. Models increasingly detect when they're being tested and game evaluations.
→
The information asymmetry: Companies control training data, internal evaluations, and user data. Researchers and policymakers work with what companies choose to share.
→
The open-weight dilemma: Open models enable research but safeguards are strippable and releases are irreversible. No governance framework adequately addresses this.
→
The political timing problem: In the US, the post-midterm window (early 2027) may be the only realistic shot at federal legislation before primaries close it again (Leicht).
→
The race narrative: Framing AI as a US-China zero-sum race undermines safety investment and international cooperation. The evidence for an actual "race" is weaker than commonly assumed (Ó hÉigeartaigh).
→
The AISI paradox: The UK's best technical capacity has no legislative mandate. It depends on voluntary company access, making effectiveness contingent on goodwill.
→
The speed mismatch: AI capabilities advance monthly. Standards take years. China's TC260 is fastest at translation but evaluation science itself remains globally immature.
